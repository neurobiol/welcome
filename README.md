# Physical Biosemiotics, Schrödinger, Quantum Biology, and Open-Endedness

## 1. What is physical biosemiotics?

**Physical biosemiotics** asks how **information, symbols, codes, and meaning can exist in physical biological systems**.

A major figure in this approach is **Howard H. Pattee**, a physicist who moved into theoretical biology.

Pattee's central question was:

> How can ordinary matter become organized so that some physical structures function as **symbols or instructions** for other physical processes?

DNA is the clearest example.

DNA is a molecule and therefore obeys chemistry and physics. But within a living system, its sequence also functions as **stored information** that is interpreted through transcription, translation, regulation, and cellular machinery.

Pattee therefore distinguished between two complementary aspects of living systems:

```text
PHYSICAL DYNAMICS                         SYMBOLIC CONSTRAINTS
─────────────────                         ────────────────────
Matter and energy                         Codes and stored information
Chemical reactions                        DNA sequences
Continuous dynamics                       Discrete symbols
Forces and reaction rates                 Rules and constraints
Phenotype                                 Genotype
```

The important idea is that **both sides are physically embodied**, but they play different roles in biological organization.

# 2. The connection to Schrödinger

In 1944, **Erwin Schrödinger** published *What Is Life?*

One of his major questions was:

> How can living organisms maintain highly organized structures and reliably transmit hereditary information while still obeying physical laws?

Schrödinger proposed that hereditary information might be stored in an **"aperiodic crystal"**: a stable molecular structure whose non-repeating arrangement could encode information.

DNA was not yet known as the hereditary molecule when he developed this argument.

Later molecular biology gave this idea a concrete physical realization.

```text
Schrödinger
"What physical structure could store hereditary information?"
              │
              ▼
Aperiodic crystal / code-script
              │
              ▼
DNA
              │
              ▼
Pattee
"How can a physical molecule function as a symbol?"
```

Pattee can therefore be viewed as continuing one part of Schrödinger's question.

Schrödinger asked how **physical matter could carry hereditary information**.

Pattee asked how that information could become part of a system in which **symbols actually control physical processes**.

---

# 3. Symbol–matter complementarity

One of Pattee's most important ideas is **symbol–matter complementarity**.

A living system cannot be described only as chemical motion.

It also contains structures whose physical states function as instructions.

For example:

```text
DNA sequence
     │
     │ interpreted by cellular machinery
     ▼
RNA
     │
     ▼
Protein
     │
     ▼
Physical action in the cell
```

The DNA sequence does not escape physics.

Instead, the living system has evolved machinery that makes particular molecular arrangements **function as instructions**.

This leads to a profound question:

> How does matter acquire the ability to constrain other matter according to stored information?

That question connects physics, biology, information theory, Artificial Life, and the origin of life.

---

# 4. Pattee and Bohr's idea of complementarity

Pattee was also influenced by **Niels Bohr's concept of complementarity**.

In quantum mechanics, different experimental arrangements can require different but complementary descriptions of the same physical system.

Pattee borrowed the broader logical idea of complementarity rather than simply claiming that biology is a quantum-mechanical measurement experiment.

He argued that living systems require two complementary descriptions:

```text
                 LIVING SYSTEM

        SYMBOLS                  DYNAMICS
           │                        │
           │                        │
      DNA / codes              Chemistry
      discrete states          motion
      descriptions             reaction rates
      constraints              physical laws
           │                        │
           └──────────┬─────────────┘
                      │
                 ORGANIZATION
```

Neither description alone gives the whole biological system.

---

# 5. The epistemic cut

Pattee called the boundary between symbolic description and physical dynamics the **epistemic cut**.

In simplified form:

```text
             THE EPISTEMIC CUT

 SYMBOL / DESCRIPTION       PHYSICAL DYNAMICS
─────────────────────       ─────────────────
DNA                         Proteins
Genotype                    Phenotype
Stored information          Physical activity
Discrete description        Continuous dynamics
Rules / constraints         Forces / reaction rates
```

The interesting feature of life is that **both sides occur inside the same physical organism**.

The cell contains:

* physical dynamics;
* stored descriptions;
* machinery that interprets those descriptions;
* physical structures produced from those descriptions.

This creates a closed relationship between **information and matter**.

---

# 6. Is this quantum biology?

## Not directly

Pattee's physical biosemiotics is **not itself a theory of quantum biology**.

Quantum biology usually investigates specific quantum phenomena in biological systems, such as:

* electron tunnelling;
* proton tunnelling;
* excitonic transport;
* spin dynamics;
* radical-pair reactions;
* quantum coherence;
* quantum effects in spectroscopy and molecular dynamics.

Physical biosemiotics instead asks about:

* symbols;
* codes;
* constraints;
* information;
* interpretation;
* organization;
* genotype–phenotype relationships.

These are different questions.

---

# 7. Where a connection can exist

There is nevertheless an interesting conceptual bridge.

```text
QUANTUM / MOLECULAR PHYSICS
            │
            ▼
Physical molecular processes
            │
            ▼
Special biological structures
            │
            ▼
Constraints on those processes
            │
            ▼
Biological function
            │
            ▼
Information / meaning / adaptation
```

Quantum biology can ask:

> What microscopic physical processes are possible?

Physical biosemiotics can ask:

> How does a living system organize and constrain physical processes so that they acquire biological function?

For example, a protein may create a very specific molecular environment in which tunnelling or coherent excitation becomes relevant.

The quantum phenomenon itself is physics.

The fact that evolution has constructed a molecular architecture that uses that phenomenon for a biological purpose belongs to a larger organizational question.

This is where the two subjects may meet.

---

# 8. Important distinction

It is important not to make the connection stronger than the evidence supports.

The following statement is reasonable:

> Biological organization can create physical environments in which quantum effects become functionally relevant.

A much stronger statement such as:

> Life exists because organisms are quantum observers.

is **not established by mainstream quantum biology**.

Pattee's use of ideas such as complementarity and the epistemic cut is primarily a conceptual framework for understanding the relationship between **physical dynamics and symbolic control**.

It should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that biological information processing requires quantum measurement or wave-function collapse.

---

# 9. Enzymes, constraints, and quantum effects

Pattee was particularly interested in **constraints**.

A physical law describes what a system can do.

A constraint restricts those possibilities.

An enzyme provides a useful example.

Without the enzyme:

```text
many possible molecular pathways
```

With the enzyme:

```text
many possibilities
       │
       ▼
specific molecular geometry
       │
       ▼
restricted reaction pathways
       │
       ▼
strong preference for particular outcomes
```

Modern quantum chemistry may be required to describe some molecular details of enzyme reactions.

Examples can include:

* electron transfer;
* proton transfer;
* tunnelling;
* electronic structure;
* transition states.

But this should be separated from the stronger philosophical claim that an enzyme literally performs a quantum measurement.

That interpretation belongs to a more speculative extension of the framework.

---

# 10. From Schrödinger to Pattee

A useful intellectual progression is:

```text
Schrödinger
      │
      │  How can physics explain hereditary order?
      ▼
Physical information storage
      │
      ▼
DNA and molecular biology
      │
      ▼
Pattee
      │
      │  How can physical structures act as symbols?
      ▼
Symbol–matter complementarity
      │
      ▼
Codes + constraints + physical dynamics
      │
      ▼
Living organization
```

Schrödinger helped shift the question toward the **physics of biological information**.

Pattee pushed the problem further toward the **physics of biological symbols and constraints**.

---

# 11. Pattee, von Neumann, and Artificial Life

Another major influence on Pattee was **John von Neumann's theory of self-reproducing automata**.

Von Neumann recognized an important logical problem.

A machine capable of constructing another machine needs both:

1. machinery that performs construction;
2. a description of what must be constructed.

This produces a structure similar to biology:

```text
DESCRIPTION                    CONSTRUCTION
───────────                    ────────────
Genetic information            Cellular machinery
DNA                            Proteins
Code                           Physical action
             │
             └──── reproduction ────┐
                                    │
                                    ▼
                              new organism
```

This connection makes Pattee especially relevant to **Artificial Life**.

ALife asks how:

* reproduction;
* heredity;
* organization;
* information;
* agency;
* evolution;

can arise in substrates other than conventional biological organisms.

# 12. Open-endedness

A further development appears in the work of **Howard Pattee and Hiroki Sayama**.

Their 2019 paper is:

> **Howard H. Pattee & Hiroki Sayama, "Evolved Open-Endedness, Not Open-Ended Evolution"**

* [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30933631/)
* [Related open-ended evolution literature](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332132174_Open-Ended_Evolution_and_Open-Endedness_Editorial_Introduction_to_the_Open-Ended_Evolution_I_Special_Issue)

The distinction in the title is important.

Instead of assuming that evolution takes place inside a permanently open-ended world, Pattee and Sayama ask how organisms themselves can **develop mechanisms that expand what they are capable of doing**.

---

# 13. What is open-endedness?

Many computational models operate inside a fixed world:

```text
fixed state variables
fixed rules
fixed interactions
fixed representation
fixed possibilities
```

Evolution can search this space extremely effectively.

But the space itself remains predefined.

Living evolution appears able to generate:

* new structures;
* new behaviors;
* new ecological relationships;
* new modes of communication;
* new ways of modifying environments;
* new mechanisms for further evolution.

So the deeper question becomes:

> How can evolution change not only the state of a system, but also the effective space of possibilities available to future evolution?

---

# 14. Evolved open-endedness

One way to summarize the idea is:

```text
Ordinary evolutionary search

Fixed possibility space
        │
        ▼
variation
        │
        ▼
selection
        │
        ▼
new states


Evolved open-endedness

Existing possibilities
        │
        ▼
evolution
        │
        ▼
new structures / constraints / representations
        │
        ▼
NEW POSSIBILITIES
        │
        ▼
further evolution
```

The system changes not only **where it is** in possibility space.

It can change aspects of the **possibility space itself**.

---

# 15. Relation to Schrödinger

There is a meaningful intellectual connection to Schrödinger, but it should be described carefully.

Schrödinger emphasized that living systems possess stable hereditary information that allows organization to persist and reproduce.

Pattee's later work asks what happens when those symbolic descriptions can produce **new physical constraints and mechanisms**.

A possible progression is:

```text
Schrödinger
      │
      ▼
How can hereditary information remain stable?
      │
      ▼
DNA / code
      │
      ▼
Pattee
      │
      ▼
How can codes constrain matter?
      │
      ▼
Pattee + Sayama
      │
      ▼
Can evolving organisms construct new constraints,
new representations and therefore new possibilities?
```

---

# 16. Is evolved open-endedness quantum biology?

## Not in the usual sense

Pattee and Sayama's 2019 work is primarily about:

* evolution;
* organization;
* representation;
* constraints;
* Artificial Life;
* novelty;
* open-ended systems.

It is **not primarily a quantum-biology paper**.

The quantum connection is mostly conceptual and historical through Pattee's broader thinking about:

* complementarity;
* measurement;
* descriptions;
* physical constraints.

A more defensible relationship is:

```text
Quantum physics
      │
      ▼
Defines microscopic physical possibilities

Biological organization
      │
      ▼
Creates structures that constrain those possibilities

Evolution
      │
      ▼
Generates new structures and constraints

Open-endedness
      │
      ▼
Creates new biological possibilities
```

Quantum mechanics defines part of the physical substrate.

Evolution can build structures that exploit that substrate.

But open-ended evolution does **not require us to assume that quantum mechanics itself is the source of biological open-endedness**.

---

# 17. Connection to quantum biology

A potentially productive research question is therefore:

> Does biological evolution sometimes create structures whose function depends specifically on quantum phenomena, and can the creation of such structures expand the functional possibilities available to living systems?

For example:

```text
Evolution
    │
    ▼
new molecular architecture
    │
    ▼
new physical constraint
    │
    ▼
control of electron / proton / excitation / spin dynamics
    │
    ▼
new biological function
```

This creates a genuine bridge between:

* evolutionary theory;
* Artificial Life;
* physical biosemiotics;
* molecular biophysics;
* quantum biology.

But the bridge should be investigated experimentally rather than assumed.

---

# 18. A larger unified question

The different ideas converge on a deeper problem:

> **How does matter acquire the ability to store descriptions, interpret information, construct constraints, remember its history, and continually create new possibilities?**

That question naturally crosses conventional disciplines:

```text
Physics
   │
   ├── What dynamics are physically possible?
   │
Chemistry
   │
   ├── What stable structures and reactions can exist?
   │
Biology
   │
   ├── Which structures become functional?
   │
Information
   │
   ├── How are states stored and transmitted?
   │
Biosemiotics
   │
   ├── How do physical states acquire biological meaning?
   │
Evolution
   │
   ├── How are new constraints generated?
   │
Artificial Life
   │
   ├── Which principles survive changes of substrate?
   │
Quantum Biology
   │
   └── When are specifically quantum physical processes functional?
```

---

# 19. Why this matters for Artificial Life

Artificial Life provides an unusually good laboratory for these questions because it allows us to construct systems in which we can deliberately vary:

* the substrate;
* the rules;
* the representation;
* the environment;
* the memory architecture;
* reproduction;
* constraints;
* communication;
* embodiment.

We can then ask:

> Which ingredients are actually necessary for life-like organization?

And beyond that:

> Which ingredients allow systems to invent genuinely new ways of organizing themselves?

This moves the problem from:

> **What does life look like?**

toward:

> **What must matter be able to do for life, agency, intelligence, and open-ended evolution to emerge?**

# Core Takeaway

The most useful connection is not:

```text
Quantum mechanics → therefore life
```

nor:

```text
Quantum measurement → therefore biological meaning
```

It is a more interesting sequence:

```text
PHYSICAL POSSIBILITY
        ↓
PHYSICAL STRUCTURE
        ↓
CONSTRAINT
        ↓
INFORMATION
        ↓
FUNCTION
        ↓
MEMORY / REPRODUCTION
        ↓
EVOLUTION
        ↓
NEW CONSTRAINTS
        ↓
NEW POSSIBILITIES
        ↓
OPEN-ENDEDNESS
```

Schrödinger asked how **physical matter could support hereditary order**.

Pattee asked how **physical matter could become both dynamics and symbolic description**.

Pattee and Sayama asked how living systems could use such organization to **continually create new possibilities**.

Quantum biology adds another question:

> **At which points in this hierarchy do specifically quantum physical phenomena become functionally important?**

That remains an open and experimentally testable scientific question.

# ABOUT THE LAST TALK ON BVSR, TELEONOMY IN THE QUANTUM BIOLOGY SESSION

> **From Darwin to Varela, and Beyond?: On BVSR as a Generative Principle for Teleonomy**

Here **BVSR** means:

**Blind Variation and Selective Retention**

The basic logic is:

```text
Generate variations
        ↓
The system does not know beforehand
which variation will succeed
        ↓
Interaction with constraints / environment
        ↓
Some variants persist
while others disappear
        ↓
Successful organization is retained
```

This idea originates with **Donald T. Campbell**, who generalized the logic of variation and selection beyond biological evolution to processes of learning and knowledge acquisition.

The important word is **blind**.

It does **not necessarily mean purely random**.

It means that, when a variation is produced, the system does not already possess complete knowledge of whether that variation will be successful.

---

# 22. From Darwinian Selection to Teleonomy

Darwinian natural selection can produce organisms that behave in apparently goal-directed ways without evolution itself needing to know the future.

This is related to the concept of **teleonomy**:

> Goal-directed or functionally organized behaviour produced by natural processes, without assuming an external designer or predetermined final goal.

A simple sequence is:

```text
variation
    ↓
selection
    ↓
retention
    ↓
organization that survives
    ↓
systems that appear increasingly fitted
to maintaining themselves
```

The interesting question is therefore:

> **Can repeated variation and retention generate the organized, apparently purposeful behaviour characteristic of living systems?**

This connects BVSR with questions about the origin of biological function and agency.

---

# 23. Why Varela Enters the Picture

**Francisco Varela and Humberto Maturana** approached life from another direction.

Their concept of **autopoiesis** emphasizes that a living system continuously produces and maintains the network of processes that makes the system itself possible.

Very roughly:

```text
DARWIN / BVSR
────────────────────
variation
selection
retention
        │
        │
        ▼
How does viable organization emerge?


VARELA / AUTOPOIESIS
────────────────────
process A enables process B
process B enables process C
process C helps regenerate process A
        │
        │
        ▼
How does an autonomous organization
maintain itself?
```

These are not identical theories.

They emphasize different problems:

* **BVSR** helps explain how viable forms can be generated and retained.
* **Autopoiesis / autonomy** asks how an existing living organization continually produces and maintains itself.

Hirota's broader work on **operational closure and autonomy** fits strongly with this second tradition.

The deeper question suggested by bringing them together is:

> **Can selection generate organizations that eventually become autonomous enough to maintain, regulate, and partly reshape the conditions of their own existence?**

That question is highly relevant to **Artificial Life**.

---

# 24. Connection to Pattee and Open-Endedness

This also connects naturally with Pattee's work.

```text
BVSR
Blind variation + selective retention
        ↓
successful organization is retained
        ↓
new constraints and structures appear
        ↓
those structures modify future possibilities
        ↓
autonomy / agency can increase
        ↓
new forms of variation become possible
        ↓
OPEN-ENDEDNESS
```

This provides a possible bridge between three questions:

1. **Campbell / BVSR:**
   How can useful organization arise without knowing the solution beforehand?

2. **Varela / autonomy:**
   How can an organization maintain and regenerate itself?

3. **Pattee / open-endedness:**
   How can evolving systems generate new constraints and therefore create new possibilities for future evolution?

Together they point toward a larger ALife question:

> **How can a system evolve from being selected by its environment to becoming an autonomous agent that also modifies the space in which future selection occurs?**

---

# 25. Is BVSR Connected to Quantum Biology?

## There is no necessary direct connection.

BVSR is a **general selection principle**.

It does not depend on:

* quantum coherence;
* quantum tunnelling;
* entanglement;
* wave-function collapse;
* quantum measurement.

Likewise, Hirota's work on **autonomy, operational closure, teleonomy, and category theory** does not by itself constitute quantum biology.

The scientifically safest relationship is:

```text
BVSR / evolution
        ↓
generates and selects biological structures
        ↓
some structures become autonomous,
regulated and functionally organized
        ↓
some of those structures may operate
using particular physical mechanisms
        ↓
in some cases those mechanisms
could involve quantum effects
```

Quantum biology therefore enters only **at the level of the physical mechanism**, if evidence shows that a selected biological function depends on a specifically quantum process.

---

# 26. A Legitimate Quantum-Biology Question

A useful research question would be:

> **Can evolutionary variation and selection produce biological structures that exploit quantum phenomena in ways that improve persistence, sensing, energy transfer, or adaptation?**

For example:

```text
variation in molecular structure
        ↓
different physical environments
        ↓
different electron / proton / spin dynamics
        ↓
selection acts on biological performance
        ↓
a structure using a quantum effect
may be retained if it provides an advantage
```

Here the quantum effect is **not the source of BVSR**.

Instead:

> **BVSR may help explain how a biological structure that exploits a quantum effect could evolve and be retained.**

That is a much stronger scientific connection than claiming that BVSR itself is quantum mechanical.

---

# 27. The Larger Connection

These ideas can therefore be placed on different explanatory levels:

```text
QUANTUM PHYSICS
What microscopic processes are possible?
        ↓
BIOPHYSICS / CHEMISTRY
What structures control those processes?
        ↓
BVSR / EVOLUTION
Which variations are retained?
        ↓
AUTONOMY / VARELA
How does the resulting organization
maintain itself?
        ↓
TELEONOMY
Why does its behaviour appear
organized toward continued existence?
        ↓
PATTEE / OPEN-ENDEDNESS
How can that organization create
new constraints and new possibilities?
        ↓
ARTIFICIAL LIFE
Can we reproduce these principles
in another substrate?
```

The important point is that these are **connected levels of explanation**, not interchangeable theories.

BVSR does not prove quantum biology.

Quantum biology does not explain teleonomy by itself.

But together they raise a powerful cross-disciplinary question:

> **How do fundamental physical possibilities become organized, selected, maintained, and eventually transformed into the apparently purposeful and open-ended behaviour of living systems?**
